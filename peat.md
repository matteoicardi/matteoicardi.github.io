# Mathematics of peatlands

Peatlands cover a few percent of the land surface but store a large share of the carbon held in soils. A peat bog is also a strange material: soft, wet, partly alive, and slowly flowing under its own weight over thousands of years. Most models treat it as a pile of carbon that grows and decays. Our work asks what changes when you take its mechanics seriously.

### Questions we work on

- **Can a bog keep growing for ever?** Mechanical limits on how thick and how fast peat can accumulate.
- **Does a peat core tell the truth?** If a bog creeps, the record in a core can be rewritten, and some histories become impossible to tell apart.
- **Why do bogs form patterns?** Pools, ridges, stripes and mazes, and whether the mechanics of the peat sets their spacing.
- **Peat as a material:** water, gas and a soft organic skeleton, from the pore scale to the landscape.

### Selected work

- A. W. Mahdiyasa, D. J. Large, M. Icardi, S. Grebby, B. P. Muljadi. *Mechanical thresholds constrain global peatland carbon accumulation.* Scientific Reports (2026).
- A. W. Mahdiyasa, D. J. Large, M. Icardi, B. P. Muljadi. *MPeat2D: a fully coupled mechanical–ecohydrological model of peatland development in two dimensions.* Earth Surface Dynamics 12 (2024).
- **Code:** [MPeat](https://github.com/MultiForm-UoN/MPeat), a long-term peatland development model with mechanics, ecology and hydrology.

### MATHPEAT network

I coordinate, with David Large, the **[MATHPEAT network](https://mathpeatnet.work/)**, supported by the Isaac Newton Institute and EPSRC, bringing together mathematicians, peatland scientists and engineers.
